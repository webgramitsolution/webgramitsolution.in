A useful ecommerce website requirements brief tells a developer what buyers and staff need to do, which business rules apply and how the result will be accepted. Begin with a sample catalogue, realistic checkout scenarios and the people responsible for product content, stock, payments and delivery. A brief can be short if the most important decisions are precise.

## Describe the business and the first release

### Define the buyers and products

List who will shop, what they buy and which regions you can serve. A retail customer may want to browse and pay immediately. A wholesale buyer may need a price enquiry or an approved account. Identify which purchase paths must launch together and which can follow later. Give examples of products with variations, bundles, limited stock and customised orders.

State the business objectives you can measure, such as completed checkouts, qualified quote requests or fewer manual order entries. Provide existing numbers if you have them. Use these objectives to prioritise the work. A first release might need a reliable catalogue and checkout before product recommendations or a loyalty programme.

### Explain what customers need to know

List required information for each product type, such as material, dimensions, ingredients or compatibility. Clarify which items need a delivery estimate or an enquiry. Give sample copy and usable images rather than a broad instruction to “make it premium.” If you want to use another site as a reference, describe the part that works for you: a clear size selector, a useful filter or a quick enquiry form.

## Prepare a representative catalogue

Give your developer five to ten genuine product examples. Include an ordinary item, a complex variant, an out-of-stock product and a product with a different shipping or tax rule. Use realistic SKU codes and category names. Explain whether the business keeps inventory by piece, pack, carton or another unit.

- Supply your current product export and identify fields that need cleanup.
- Decide who owns product text, prices, stock and image rights.
- Identify filters buyers depend on, such as size, style or material compatibility.
- Include a photo with known cropping constraints and confirm the preferred mobile presentation.
- Flag products that require an enquiry or quote before purchase.

Ask someone from merchandising to review the catalogue after a sample import. Compare the source data with what customers see and check whether staff can edit it confidently. Set aside time for corrections to descriptions, missing images and variant relationships before the full import.

## Write checkout rules as testable outcomes

### Include delivery and payments that apply in India

Document the serviceable PIN codes, courier rules, pickup options and charges. Decide what happens when a shopper provides an address beyond your service area. List the payment methods you plan to offer and confirm gateway eligibility with the provider. Agree whether shipping and taxes are shown before payment, and how the team generates invoices with the required details.

Use outcome-based wording. For example, a shopper with an eligible PIN code should see their delivery charge before submitting payment. A cancelled payment should leave them able to return to checkout without causing a duplicate order. An item marked as unavailable should explain whether the buyer can request an alert or place a backorder.

### Define returns and customer service

List return windows, exclusions and who approves a refund. Specify which statuses customers receive by email or in their account. Describe the escalation path when a courier return and a payment refund occur on different days. A clear operating procedure matters because the website reflects business decisions that support teams must carry out.

## Make integration ownership explicit

Identify the source system for product data, orders and stock. For each integration, write what events are sent, who supplies access, how updates are tested and who receives alerts when an update fails. A single owner should be able to answer what happens to a partially fulfilled order or a failed refund notification.

Share anonymised sample data or sandbox access where possible. Confirm whether existing systems have a supported connector or API and whether development depends on another supplier. If the timeline depends on gateway approval or a courier account, record the dependency and define what can be tested before external access arrives.

## Outline migration, SEO and accessibility

For an existing site, provide the sitemap and sample URLs that already attract search traffic. Identify account data, historical orders and product records that must be retained. Agree who creates redirects and which information needs manual verification before publishing. For a new store, decide categories, naming conventions and product page fields so content stays consistent as it grows.

Ask for mobile testing, keyboard navigation and understandable form errors. Choose tests that show whether buyers can complete their goals. For example, review what an error message says when an address is unsupported and check whether a keyboard user can open and close filters and proceed to checkout.

## Example of an acceptance-ready brief

A homeware retailer plans a launch for 150 products across two cities. Its catalogue owner supplies clean SKU records, images and stock each week. The site should accept a prepaid purchase with delivery charges and allow pickup for selected locations. A support lead must see cancelled and refunded orders in a report. For oversized items, a customer must be able to request a quote with dimensions rather than check out at a standard price.

Define who signs off on each outcome. For the prepaid test, verify the order and confirmation in the storefront and gateway. For pickup, confirm the branch can identify the order and the customer receives instructions. Use those scenarios in the quotation so vendors estimate the same work.

## Finalise the brief before requesting quotes

- List launch requirements separately from ideas that can wait.
- Name the content owner, technical contact and final approver.
- Provide the budget range, timing and known dependencies.
- Set a short checklist for approval, launch and handover.
- Note assumptions that may change, such as adding a second dispatch warehouse.

Keep a shared copy of the approved brief and update it when decisions change. Record the person responsible and the reason for each change. This gives a developer a usable project reference and helps your team spot gaps before work begins.

### Document who handles hard-to-reproduce issues

Ask who should confirm a defect when it affects only one product, payment method or device. Include a way to capture the scenario and the expected outcome without sharing unnecessary personal information. This helps your vendor estimate the amount of testing and support required.

## Frequently asked questions

### What should I put in an ecommerce website brief?
Include your buyers, catalogue examples, shipping and payment rules, integration requirements, launch priorities and acceptance tests. Name the people who provide inputs and sign off.

### Do I need every product ready before speaking with a developer?
A representative product sample is enough for an initial discussion. Identify who will prepare the full catalogue and when it will be ready for import and review.

### How do I describe a feature clearly?
Write an observable customer or staff outcome, then include a normal case and an exception. Explain which system records the result and who should be notified.

### How should I handle features that are still undecided?
Document the assumption used in the quote and set a date to resolve it. Features that can wait should be scoped separately from day-one requirements.
