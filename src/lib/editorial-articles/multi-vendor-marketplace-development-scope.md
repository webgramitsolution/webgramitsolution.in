Before building a multi-vendor marketplace, decide who owns the order, who fulfils it and where the money flows. A site that introduces buyers to suppliers is a different operating model from a site that takes payment for several sellers in one checkout. Choose the model first so the product scope reflects what your operations, finance and support teams can run.

## Choose the marketplace model

### Directory, quote marketplace or transactional platform

A directory helps buyers find suppliers and sends them to a contact or enquiry flow. It needs reliable seller information, useful product discovery and a plan for qualifying leads. A quote marketplace can collect a buyer request, route it to suitable sellers and track responses. It may need quotation versions, approval rules and a record of who accepted the terms.

A transactional marketplace handles orders and may collect payments, calculate commission or coordinate payouts. This adds responsibilities for refunds, disputes, order status and reconciliation. Verify payment-provider capabilities and clarify the applicable tax, invoicing and regulatory obligations with your advisers. Avoid choosing a settlement flow based only on how another site appears to work to buyers.

### Set rules for the first category

Choose one initial category and describe a typical transaction. Industrial products may require specifications and quotes before a price is final. Lifestyle products may suit a standard cart, but variants, stock and returns still need clear ownership. Test the model with a small set of sellers and identify how much manual work the operator can realistically handle.

## Scope seller onboarding and catalogue approval

Decide what seller information is required, who reviews it and what a seller can do while approval is pending. Confirm where documents are stored, who can see them and when the business needs to request updates. Give seller support a way to explain why a submission needs more detail. Record the decision and who made it.

Define the product data model before letting sellers add inventory. Some catalogues allow each seller to create new products. Others use one shared product reference and let sellers submit offers against it. The right approach depends on how buyers compare items and whether duplicate listings help or confuse them.

- Decide who owns names, images, product descriptions and categories.
- Require the specifications needed for search, comparison and safe fulfilment.
- Review the rules for publishing and editing regulated or restricted products.
- Align stock units and pack sizes before accepting bulk quantities.
- Define how sellers correct rejected products and who approves the revision.

## Design orders and fulfilment across multiple sellers

### Work through a two-seller cart

Imagine a buyer chooses two items sold by different suppliers. Decide whether the buyer experiences one cart or separate enquiries. If there is one checkout, plan how each seller receives their portion, which items can ship separately and what the buyer sees when one item is delayed. Record who can cancel or amend each part of the order.

Specify what happens when a seller’s stock count is wrong or shipping a specific PIN code is unavailable. A clear status should tell the buyer what can still be delivered and whether a refund or alternative is due. Give support access to the order timeline without exposing sensitive records from other sellers.

### Assign ownership of returns

Agree which party approves returns, funds a refund, receives the goods and resolves disputes. Define what happens when the two sellers have different return rules, or a promotional discount applies to the whole cart. Work with your legal and operations teams on buyer communications and the terms that must be visible before purchase.

## Define commissions and payout reconciliation

A commission may be based on item value, order value, category, seller agreement or a combination. Decide what happens to discounts, shipping, tax and fees in the calculation. Make the payout record explain the amount with a clear order reference and adjustments. Agree who approves exceptions and how sellers can raise a discrepancy.

Settle the practical questions before writing the integration. When are funds considered available? Is the timing related to dispatch, delivery or the end of a return period? Who owns a negative balance after a later refund? Review payout restrictions and applicable processes with the chosen payment provider. Give finance a reconciliation export and use realistic sample transactions to verify totals.

## Prioritise the seller and operator experience

Give sellers a small set of tasks they can perform without assistance, such as editing stock, acknowledging an order and opening a support request. Provide the operator with views for approvals, unresolved disputes and failed fulfilment. Name who receives alerts and how they decide which exception is urgent. Avoid forcing staff to depend on scattered emails for decisions that should be recorded with the order.

For the launch, keep delivery rules and commission models narrow enough to test. A pilot can include seller onboarding, a small catalogue and manually reviewed exceptions. Track repeat exceptions during the pilot. Use that evidence to decide whether more automation is needed, rather than assuming that every manual step must be eliminated on day one.

## Acceptance checks before release

- Confirm a seller cannot view another seller’s private orders or documents.
- Verify product approval and editing with both allowed and rejected changes.
- Test a two-seller order, a partial cancellation and one partial refund.
- Reconcile buyer payment, commission and seller payout against the invoices.
- Confirm fulfilment messages and escalation workflows on mobile and desktop.

A marketplace scope is ready when the buyer experience, seller responsibilities and financial records agree in a full test transaction. Save the approved scenarios and ask operations and finance to sign off on what the platform will support at launch.

### Define what your marketplace promises sellers

Set expectations around dispatch time, customer messages and payouts before approving a seller. Use a simple scorecard for fulfilment issues such as late shipment or inaccurate stock. Decide which signals require a warning, a temporary pause or manual review. For a quote marketplace, track the time to first useful seller response and whether the buyer can compare proposals in the same units. Offer sellers a clear route to dispute inaccurate records. If early sellers struggle with the same task, adjust seller training and catalogue rules before adding more features. Review the results with the people responsible for supporting sellers after launch.

During the pilot, keep a written log of seller questions and operator interventions. It can reveal missing explanations about delivery fees, proof of dispatch or payout timing that an order demo with ideal data would miss.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does every marketplace need checkout and automated seller payouts?
No. A directory or quote marketplace may start with supplier discovery and enquiries. Choose transactional checkout only when your team can operate the required payment and fulfilment procedures.

### How should marketplace commissions be calculated?
Define a base amount, applicable fees and exclusions. Verify how discounts, shipping, taxes and partial refunds affect the final amount your seller receives.

### Who should manage returns?
Write down who receives the returned item, approves the refund and responds to buyers. Test the workflow with more than one seller before going live.

### What should a marketplace pilot include?
Use a real category, a small set of sellers and representative orders. Test onboarding, stock changes, fulfilment and at least one exception before broadening the scope.
